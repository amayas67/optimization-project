A Concrete Problem: The Frequency Allocation Dilemma

A telecommunications regulator must assign radio frequencies to six new broadcast stations. Two stations too close to each other cannot share a frequency — or their signals interfere.

The regulator has drawn a map of interference. Each conflict between two stations is a constraint that must be respected:

A ↔ BA ↔ C B ↔ CB ↔ D B ↔ EC ↔ E D ↔ F

Seven pairwise conflicts across six stations — the graph shown above, where a line joins two stations that cannot share a frequency. The regulator's goal is simple to state, yet difficult to achieve: assign frequencies using as few distinct values as possible. Why the minimum? Because every frequency band is a scarce, expensive resource. Using 5 frequencies when only 3 would suffice means wasting spectrum that could serve emergency channels, mobile networks, or future broadcasters.

This situation is not hypothetical. It is the Frequency Assignment Problem (FAP), encountered daily by telecommunications regulators worldwide. The same structure appears in university timetabling (minimize exam slots), register allocation in compilers (minimize hardware registers), and cartography (the famous four-color theorem). In every case, the core challenge is identical: partition objects into groups such that conflicting objects land in different groups, using the smallest number of groups possible.

02

From Stations to Graphs

To reason rigorously, we translate the concrete situation into an abstract model. Each station becomes a vertex. Each interference link becomes an edge. Each frequency becomes a color.

Definition

Coloring & Chromatic Number

A valid coloring is an assignment of integers (colors) to vertices such that no edge connects two vertices of the same color. The chromatic number, denoted χ(G), is the smallest integer k for which such a coloring exists.

The diagram in the header illustrates exactly such an answer: three frequencies are enough to color all six stations without a single conflicting edge. This is not a coincidence. Stations A, B, and C mutually interfere with one another, forming a closed triangle of conflicts that, on its own, already requires three distinct frequencies — no triangle can be colored with fewer. Three is therefore both achievable for the whole network and provably optimal: this is exactly what χ(G) = 3 means.

Stripped of its application-specific dressing, the regulator's map is a graph G = (V, E) — and the question becomes purely mathematical.

Why the Minimum Is Non-Negotiable

One might ask: why not settle for a "good enough" coloring? In practice, the gap between suboptimal and optimal carries tangible consequences.

Spectrum economics

In exam timetabling, every extra slot extends the examination period, disrupting logistics for thousands of students and staff.

In compiler design, exceeding the physical register count spills data to slower memory, degrading the performance of the generated code.

The Core Problem

Frequency allocation, exam scheduling, register allocation, map coloring: every one of these situations reduces to the same challenge once translated into a graph.

Problem Statement

The Graph Coloring Problem — how can a graph be colored so as to guarantee, or at least closely approximate, the absolute minimum number of colors possible?

The next chapter takes this abstract question and grounds it in a single, concrete instance — where the gap between a valid coloring and the optimal coloring becomes visible, and measurable.
