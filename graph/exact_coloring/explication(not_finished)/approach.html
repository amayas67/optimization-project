The Instance: A Bipartite Graph

Six vertices split into two groups X = {A, B, C} and Y = {D, E, F}, connected by six edges. Each edge links a vertex in X to a vertex in Y; the three "obvious" edges A–D, B–E, and C–F are missing. This graph will serve as our guiding thread from start to finish.

Reading the image — and the ground truth

The circles represent vertices; the gray lines are edges; the coral dashes mark the three missing edges (the graph is K₃,₃ minus a perfect matching). No edge connects two vertices in the same column — this is precisely what makes the graph bipartite, and it guarantees that the chromatic number is χ = 2. Keep this number in mind: it is the ground truth against which we will evaluate every strategy hereafter.

02

Order, Colors, and Explosion

Coloring a graph amounts to making two fundamental decisions, vertex by vertex: in what order to process the vertices, and with which color to paint them.

Our ultimate goal is to find the sequence of orders and color choices that yields the minimum number of colors possible — the chromatic number, here χ = 2. For this 6-vertex graph, without constraints, each vertex could receive one of 6 colors, giving 6⁶ = 46,656 raw assignments. A modern machine sweeps through this number in the blink of an eye — this is precisely why this small instance is a good case study: we can still verify the ground truth by exhaustive enumeration.

However, the formula is kⁿ, and it explodes. At 20 vertices with 20 colors, the count already reaches approximately 10²⁶ — well beyond any conceivable enumeration. Even with aggressive pruning, the search remains exponential in n in the worst case.

The first finding: the impossibility of enumeration. Brute force is definitively ruled out for large graphs.
03

The Local Metric Trap

Our first reflex is the greedy algorithm: a fixed order, always the smallest available color. Will it reach the optimum? It all depends on the order — watch the two graphs color themselves in real time, vertex by vertex.

Step 0 / 6
Order 1 · A→D→B→E→C→Fcolors: –

    Order 2 · A→E→C→D→B→Fcolors: –

      Color 0 Color 1 Color 2 (wasted) not yet colored

      Look at the second step: the Order 1 graph still uses only a single color, while Order 2 already requires two. A greedy observer would bet on Order 1. Yet the final result is reversed: Order 1 ends at 3 colors, Order 2 stays at 2 — the true chromatic number.

      The second finding: relying on the current color count is deeply misleading. Order 1 looks better until step 4, before collapsing.

      The Limits of Heuristics

      The scientific community has developed a whole family of algorithms, each embodying a different philosophy about where to look first.

      AlgorithmThe ideaExact?
      GreedyProcesses vertices in a fixed order; gives each the smallest available color.No
      Random GreedyRuns the greedy algorithm on k random orders and keeps the best result.No
      Welsh–PowellGreedy, but processes highest-degree vertices first.No
      DSATURAlways colors the most "saturated" vertex — the one whose neighbors already use the most distinct colors.No
      IDOIncidence Degree Ordering: colors the vertex with the most colored neighbors; breaks ties by total degree.No
      RLFRecursive Largest First: builds color classes one by one, greedily selecting the vertex with the most uncolored neighbors.No

      Beyond Bipartite: The Clover

      The bipartite graph was just a warm-up. Here is a richer adversary: non-bipartite, eight vertices, three nested triangles, χ = 3 — and a greedy that stumbles where the seven other algorithms succeed.

      The three triangles
      Visual representation

      Hover a vertex — or a triangle on the left — to highlight them.

      Reading the image — three triangles, χ = 3

      The Clover takes its name from its three triangles — A–B–E, B–C–D, and B–D–E — which share their vertices around the hinge edge B–D. Three triangles, therefore χ ≥ 3; and a 3-coloring exists (B=0, C=1, D=2, A=2, E=1, F=0, G=1, H=0), so χ = 3. Note that A and D, though non-adjacent, share color 2 in this coloring — this is precisely what the greedy will miss. Vertices F, G, H form a harmless tail for χ, but give the heuristics more room to diverge.

          Anatomy of the trap

          The greedy's state at step 5 — E (pulsed) sees A=0, B=1, D=2.

          The trap is subtle. A and D are not adjacent: an optimal coloring can give them the same color. But the greedy froze A=0 at the very first step, without knowing that D would end up as 2. Result: when it comes time to color E, its three neighbors A, B, and D exhibit three distinct colors, and E — adjacent to all three — has no choice but to invent a fourth color.

          This is richer than the bipartite trap: here, it's three nested triangles that channel the colors of B, D, and E toward three distinct values, before the F–G–H tail confirms the damage.

          The greedy doesn't go wrong at step 5. It goes wrong at step 1 — by freezing A without knowing what the structure would demand later.
          06

          Locating the Ideal Strategy

          The fundamental question remains intact: can we choose the order and the colors without testing everything?

          We must acknowledge this rigorously: we do not know the answer. It is, in fact, an open problem. Our approach is therefore conditional: if such a way of doing things (fixed or dynamic) exists, then we should be able to locate it within our search space.

          But how to locate it? By using filters. A filter designates a property, a structural condition, that this ideal way of doing things necessarily satisfies, but that other candidates do not. The successive application of these filters eliminates suboptimal strategies, drastically reducing the search space and potentially allowing us to converge toward this way of doing things.

          The value of intuition. A "rule" is in reality just a word that encompasses all possible ways of filtering our search space. But since there is an infinity of possible ways to filter, we must once again be selective. Hence the value of intuition: we need to propose families of filters motivated by structural understanding, rather than attempting to explore the unexplorable.
